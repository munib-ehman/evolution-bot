Someone lands on your product page, scrolls halfway down, pauses, then closes the tab. You never see it happen. No email, no phone call, just a visitor who had a question nobody answered in time. That moment repeats itself hundreds of times a month on most small stores, and almost none of it shows up in your analytics as a "problem" you can point to.

Proactive live chat is built for exactly that gap: catching a visitor while they're still on the page, not after they've already left.

## What proactive chat actually means

Reactive chat waits for the customer to click the bubble and type first. Proactive chat triggers a message based on behavior: time spent on a page, scroll depth, an item added to cart and then removed, or the cursor moving toward the top of the browser (a common exit-intent signal). Instead of "Hi, how can I help?" sitting passively in the corner, the store starts the conversation at the moment hesitation is most likely.

Industry data on this is now fairly consistent: stores running behavior-triggered chat report 15 to 35% higher conversion on the sessions where it fires, and recovery rates on carts assisted by proactive chat run 20 to 25% higher than carts left to abandon silently. That's not a reason to chat with every visitor. It's a reason to chat with the right ones, at the right second.

## The four triggers worth setting up first

You don't need a dozen rules. Four cover most of the real hesitation moments on a small store.

### 1. Time on a high-intent page

If someone has been on a product page for 45 to 60 seconds without adding to cart, they're reading, comparing, or stuck on a question your page doesn't answer. A soft trigger here, something like "Questions about sizing or shipping? I'm here," outperforms a generic greeting because it names the likely blocker.

### 2. Cart abandonment in progress

Item added, then user navigates back to browsing or opens a new tab without checking out. This is a stronger signal than time on page alone, since intent has already been shown once. A message referencing the actual item in the cart ("Still deciding on the [product name]? Happy to answer anything before you check out") converts noticeably better than a generic nudge.

### 3. Exit intent on checkout

If a visitor is on the checkout page and the cursor moves toward the browser's back or close button, that's the last real chance to catch a question about shipping cost, return policy, or payment security before you lose the sale entirely. This overlaps with the work of [fixing checkout friction](/blog/reduce-abandoned-checkouts-shopify) directly, since a lot of exit-intent moments trace back to a surprise cost or a missing trust signal.

### 4. Repeat visits without purchase

Someone back on your site for the third time this week and still hasn't bought is a warmer lead than almost any other segment. A trigger that recognizes returning, non-purchasing visitors and opens with something specific ("Welcome back, still thinking it over?") beats cold outreach every time, because the visitor already knows the brand.

## What to say when the chat opens

The trigger gets attention. The message decides whether it converts or gets closed immediately.

- Keep the first line under 15 words. Nobody reads a paragraph in a chat popup.
- Name the likely hesitation instead of asking an open question. "Free shipping over $75, want the code?" beats "Can I help you with anything?"
- Never lead with a discount as the first move. Lead with the answer to the question they probably have, and offer the discount only if they're still stuck.
- Set a visible fallback for when nobody's watching chat live. A bot handling FAQs about shipping, sizing, and returns, with a clear path to a human for anything else, keeps the trigger useful 24 hours a day instead of only during your working hours.

## The staffing problem this creates

Here's the part most stores underestimate: proactive triggers only work if something is actually watching the chat, and a small store owner cannot sit refreshing a chat widget between order fulfillment and everything else on their plate. This is the same tradeoff covered in [AI customer support pricing](/blog/ai-customer-support-pricing-per-resolution): the honest option for a one or two person team is an AI layer that handles the first response instantly, resolves what it can on its own (sizing, order status, shipping timing), and escalates only the conversations that genuinely need a human judgment call.

Done well, this doesn't feel like a bot deflecting you. It feels like someone was already there. That's the actual bar: would a customer notice, or would they just get their answer and move on with checking out.

## A realistic example

A store selling skincare at roughly $60 average order value set a single trigger: 40 seconds on any product page, plus a second trigger on cart abandonment referencing the specific item. Over six weeks, chat-assisted sessions converted at close to double the site average, and abandoned cart recovery on carts that received a chat message ran about 22% higher than carts that didn't. No discount was involved in either trigger, just faster answers to questions the product page wasn't already answering, which ties back to broader [conversational commerce](/blog/conversational-commerce-for-small-stores) work many small stores are only now starting to take seriously.

## Where to start if you have nothing set up

1. Turn on one trigger first: cart abandonment in progress. It's the highest-intent moment and the easiest to write a message for.
2. Write three canned responses for your most common presale questions (shipping time, return policy, sizing or fit) so whatever answers the trigger, human or AI, isn't improvising.
3. Watch two weeks of transcripts. The questions that keep coming up are usually questions your product pages should be answering anyway, which is worth fixing at the source too.
4. Add the exit-intent checkout trigger once the first one is running cleanly. Don't stack all four triggers on day one, it gets noisy fast for both you and your visitors.

Proactive chat isn't about chatting with everyone. It's about showing up in the handful of moments a visitor is actually deciding whether to buy, and most small stores are leaving those moments completely unattended.
