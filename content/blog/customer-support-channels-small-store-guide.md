At some point every store owner ends up managing email, Instagram DMs, a contact form, maybe live chat, and a phone number nobody remembers to check, all at once. Adding a channel because a competitor has it feels productive. Checking five inboxes a day when you're also packing orders is not.

The question isn't "which channels exist." It's which ones your actual customers use, and which ones you can realistically staff without burning your week.

## Start with where your customers already are, not where support tools want you to be

Every helpdesk vendor will tell you to run omnichannel from day one: email, chat, SMS, social, phone. That's built for teams with a support staff, not a one or two person store. The better starting question is: where are people already trying to reach you?

Check three things before adding anything:

- Your email inbox volume over the last 30 days, and what percentage are presale vs. post-purchase questions
- Your Instagram and Facebook DMs, since a lot of small stores get real purchase questions here that never make it to a "real" support channel
- Whether people are commenting on posts asking questions that should be DMs or emails, a sign your current channels aren't discoverable enough

Most small stores find 80% of contact volume sits in one or two channels, usually email and one social platform. That's where to invest first, not everywhere at once.

## The core channels, ranked by what a small store actually needs

### Email: the non-negotiable baseline

Every store needs a monitored support email, full stop. It's asynchronous, doesn't need real-time staffing, and customers expect it regardless of what else you offer. The failure mode here isn't having email, it's response time. 43% of customers expect a reply within 24 hours, and letting a support inbox sit for three days is the single most common way small stores lose repeat customers without realizing it.

### Live chat: only worth it if you can staff or automate it

Chat converts presale questions into sales better than almost any other channel, since it catches someone at the exact moment they're deciding whether to buy. The catch is that chat left unanswered for hours does more damage to trust than not having chat at all, an empty bubble sitting on your site all day looks worse than no bubble. If you can't staff it live, an AI layer that resolves shipping, sizing, and order-status questions instantly and escalates the rest is the realistic middle ground, not a compromise most customers even notice, as covered in [AI customer support pricing](/blog/ai-customer-support-pricing-per-resolution).

### Social DMs: treat it like a real channel, because customers do

Customers increasingly ask "does this come in blue" or "is this true to size" directly in a comment or DM rather than emailing, especially stores that sell through Instagram or TikTok content. If you're not checking DMs at least once a day, you're losing sales you never even see as lost, since the customer just moves on to the next store in their feed.

### Phone: usually not worth setting up from scratch

Unless you sell a high-consideration or high-ticket product where buyers want to talk before purchasing, phone support for a small ecommerce store is mostly overhead. It requires real-time staffing, doesn't scale, and most customers under 40 actively prefer not to call. Skip it unless you have specific evidence customers are asking for it.

### SMS: for order updates, not open-ended support

SMS earns its place for transactional updates like shipping notifications and back-in-stock alerts, where open rates are extremely high and the message is short. It's a poor fit for open-ended support conversations, since customers don't expect to type out a complex return question in a text thread.

## A simple channel map for a $50k-$1M store

| Channel | Staff it live? | Automate it? |
|---|---|---|
| Email | No, 24hr response window is fine | Yes, for FAQs and order status |
| Live chat | Only during peak hours if at all | Yes, AI-first with human escalation |
| Social DMs | Check daily minimum | Partial, route obvious FAQs |
| SMS | No | Yes, transactional only |
| Phone | Skip unless requested | N/A |

## Why consolidation beats adding more channels

The real cost of a scattered channel setup isn't the tools, it's the context switching. Answering the same "where's my order" question in email, then DMs, then chat, three separate times a day, is the kind of busywork that [quietly eats a founder's week](/blog/hiring-va-vs-automating-shopify-store) without ever showing up as a line item. Most WISMO-type questions can be [resolved before they ever reach an inbox](/blog/wismo-cut-support-tickets-in-half) if the answer is available proactively, which cuts ticket volume across every channel at once rather than just moving the problem around.

The math tends to favor fewer, better-run channels over more channels run thin. A [cost comparison against hiring support help](/blog/gorgias-vs-diy-support-cost-math) usually shows the same thing: the expensive part was never the channel itself, it was the hours spent manually answering the same handful of questions across all of them.

## What to actually do this week

1. Pull 30 days of support volume by channel. If you don't track this, start now, it's the only way to know where to invest.
2. Kill or ignore whichever channel gets under 5% of volume, rather than trying to staff it properly.
3. Set a 24-hour response standard on email and stick to it, even if that means a canned acknowledgment while you work the real answer.
4. If chat or DMs are your highest-intent channel, look at automating the first response there before adding anything new.

You don't need every channel. You need the two or three your customers actually use, answered fast and consistently.
