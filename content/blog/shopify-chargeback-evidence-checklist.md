A good Shopify chargeback response is not the largest pile of screenshots you can upload. It is a short, ordered case that answers the bank's specific dispute reason with records tied to the exact order.

Start with the due date and the customer's claim. Then build a timeline, attach the most relevant proof, explain any apparent contradiction, and submit before the deadline. If the charge is genuinely wrong, accept it rather than trying to bury the problem in documents.

This checklist is for merchants using Shopify Payments. A dispute handled by PayPal or another payment provider can have different screens, deadlines, and evidence rules, so follow that provider's process.

## Triage the dispute before collecting evidence

Open the disputed order and record five facts in one working note:

1. the response due date shown in Shopify
2. the dispute type and the issuer's claim, when available
3. the disputed amount and transaction date
4. the fulfillment, delivery, refund, and support timeline
5. the one question your evidence needs to answer

That last point keeps the response focused. A “product not received” dispute asks whether the customer received what they bought. A “fraudulent transaction” dispute asks whether the cardholder authorized the purchase. A “product unacceptable” dispute asks whether the delivered product matched what was promised. The same attachment does not answer all three.

Shopify's current [chargeback process documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/chargebacks/chargeback-process) says the response window is limited—usually 7 to 21 days—and that the customer's bank and card company, not Shopify, decide the outcome. Use the date displayed on your order as the operative deadline.

## Build a one-page case summary

Before uploading files, write a factual summary that a reviewer can understand without knowing your store.

Use this structure:

> Order 1048 was placed on September 3 for one blue travel bag. The payment passed the verification recorded on the order. We shipped it to the checkout address on September 4. Carrier tracking 123456 shows delivery at that address on September 7. The customer asked about the delivery location on September 8, and we replied with the carrier's delivery photo and claim process. No refund was issued.

Replace every detail with the order's actual record. Do not guess who used the card, say a package was signed for when it was not, or describe a customer message that you cannot attach.

A useful summary includes:

- order number, date, items, and total
- customer name and the addresses supplied at checkout
- payment-verification facts visible in the order
- fulfillment and delivery milestones
- refund, replacement, cancellation, or support history
- a direct sentence explaining why the attached evidence answers the dispute

Keep emotion out of it. “The customer is obviously lying” proves nothing. “The attached carrier record shows delivery to the checkout address at 2:14 p.m.” points the reviewer to verifiable evidence.

## Match evidence to the dispute reason

Use the smallest complete set for the claim in front of you.

| Dispute reason | Evidence that usually matters |
|---|---|
| Fraudulent transaction | AVS/CVV results shown on the order, 3D Secure record when present, matching IP or device history, prior undisputed orders, delivery to a verified address, customer messages acknowledging the purchase |
| Product not received | Carrier, tracking number, delivery status, delivery address, signature or delivery photo when available, delivery notifications, and customer correspondence |
| Product unacceptable | Product page as it appeared at purchase, variant and order details, pre-shipment or quality-control records, return policy, and the support thread showing the resolution offered |
| Duplicate charge | Transaction IDs and timestamps, separate receipts or order numbers, an explanation of authorization versus captured charge, or refund confirmation |
| Subscription canceled | Subscription terms accepted at purchase, renewal notice where applicable, cancellation method, billing and usage history, and the customer's cancellation messages |
| Incorrect amount or general dispute | Itemized receipt, checkout total, tax/shipping/fee breakdown, currency shown, transaction record, and relevant customer messages |

This mapping follows Shopify's current [evidence guidance by dispute type](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/chargebacks/chargeback-process). Only submit evidence your records support.

## The evidence-packet checklist

Work through the packet in this order.

### 1. Transaction and order record

Include the itemized order, transaction status, timestamps, and the verification results Shopify displays. Make the order number and disputed amount easy to find.

Do not turn a weak fraud case into a strong-sounding one. An address mismatch is not something to omit and hope the reviewer misses; it is a risk fact to understand. Your broader [fraud-review process](/blog/ai-fraud-detection-for-shopify-stores) should determine which orders you fulfill before a dispute ever happens.

### 2. The product or offer as sold

Save the product description, variant, price, fulfillment promise, and policy version that applied when the order was placed. A current product page is weak evidence if you changed it after the purchase. For subscriptions or digital products, include the terms and the record of access or use.

### 3. Fulfillment and delivery proof

Provide carrier, tracking number, ship date, destination, delivery date, and any signature or delivery image. Make sure the attachment shows the address relationship rather than only a “Delivered” label with no destination.

If the shipment was late, lost, or delivered to the wrong place, say so. A clean resolution through proactive [order and shipping updates](/blog/order-shipping-update-emails-customers-read) is usually better than defending an indefensible fulfillment record.

### 4. Customer communication

Export or capture the complete relevant thread with dates, sender identities, and enough context to follow the conversation. Highlight acknowledgements such as the customer discussing the order, confirming receipt, requesting a size exchange, or accepting a replacement.

Do not crop a message so tightly that its meaning changes. If the customer reported a real problem, include the response and remedy you offered. The goal is a reliable timeline, not selective storytelling.

### 5. Refund and resolution history

Show refunds, store credit, replacements, return labels, or cancellation attempts. Reconcile amounts: if you issued a partial refund, explain what it covered and what amount remains disputed.

Once a chargeback has started, Shopify says the order payment cannot be refunded through Shopify because the bank is handling the disputed funds. The [Shopify admin chargeback guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/chargebacks/chargebacks-in-admin) also explains how to save, submit, or accept a response. Do not promise a second refund without understanding the status of the first dispute.

### 6. A concise closing statement

End with the requested finding and the evidence that supports it:

> The order was authorized and delivered to the address supplied at checkout. We ask that the dispute be resolved in the merchant's favor. The transaction verification, carrier delivery record, and customer's post-delivery message are attached.

Change that sentence if the facts do not support it.

## What not to submit

More files can make a response harder to review. Leave out:

- unrelated policy pages or an entire website export
- internal opinions about the customer
- unsupported claims about the customer's identity or motive
- duplicate screenshots of the same fact
- generic fraud scores with no explanation of the underlying order signals
- edited images that obscure dates, addresses, or context
- personal data that is not needed to answer the claim

Name attachments by function, such as `01-order-record.pdf`, `02-tracking-delivery.pdf`, and `03-customer-thread.pdf`. Check that every file opens and remains readable at normal zoom.

## Decide when to accept instead of fight

Accepting a valid chargeback is not the same as ignoring it. It is an explicit conclusion that the store's evidence does not support keeping the funds.

Acceptance may be the honest choice when:

- you charged the customer twice and did not correct it
- the order was never fulfilled
- tracking shows delivery somewhere other than the checkout address
- the customer canceled within the agreed terms but was still billed
- the item materially differed from the product page and no adequate remedy was provided
- the amount is already owed back and the dispute is not merely an inquiry

If the dispute exposes a service failure, fix the process after closing the case. A clear [returns and exchanges workflow](/blog/returns-without-losing-the-customer) can resolve many product problems before the customer turns to their bank.

## Run a 30-minute postmortem

After submission, tag the dispute by root cause rather than only by bank reason code:

- unrecognized statement descriptor
- fraud screening miss
- fulfillment delay or bad tracking
- delivery proof gap
- product-page mismatch
- subscription cancellation failure
- slow or inconsistent support
- duplicate or incorrect charge

Then assign one preventive action, an owner, and a due date. Examples include adding the store name customers recognize to receipt copy, requiring signature for a defined high-risk order class, archiving product-page versions, or tightening cancellation confirmation.

Do not promise that any checklist will win a dispute. The issuer decides, and complete evidence can still lose. The controllable part is whether your response is timely, relevant, factual, and easy to verify.

For the next open dispute, start by writing the one-page timeline. If you cannot state the case cleanly from your own records, the evidence gap is the first operational problem to fix.
