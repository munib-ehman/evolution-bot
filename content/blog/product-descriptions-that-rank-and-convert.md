Most product descriptions are written once, fast, and never touched again. They're a list of features nobody asked about, written to fill space under a photo rather than to answer the question a real customer actually has before buying.

That's a shame, because the product description is one of the few pieces of content on your store that can do two jobs at once: rank in search and close the sale. Most stores get neither, because they write for neither.

## Why generic descriptions fail at both jobs

A description like "Made from premium materials, this versatile piece is perfect for any occasion" fails at SEO because it contains no keyword a real person would search for, and it fails at conversion because it answers nothing. It's the retail equivalent of small talk: technically words, but not information.

Search engines want specific, relevant language that matches what people actually type. Buyers want specific, relevant language that answers what they're actually wondering. It's the same fix for both problems: be specific.

## Start with the actual question, not the feature list

Before writing anything, write down the three questions a real customer asks right before adding this product to cart. For a lot of small stores this looks like:

- Will this fit / work for my specific situation?
- How is this different from the cheaper version?
- What happens if it doesn't work out?

Your description should answer these in plain language, in the first few lines, before diving into specs. If someone has to scroll past three paragraphs of brand story to find out the sizing runs small, you've already lost some of them.

### A worked example

Compare two descriptions for the same product, a ceramic pour-over coffee dripper:

**Generic version:** "Elevate your morning routine with our beautifully crafted ceramic dripper. Made with care, this elegant piece is a must-have for coffee lovers."

**Specific version:** "Fits any mug or carafe between 4-8 inches wide. The ceramic holds heat better than plastic drippers, so your coffee doesn't cool mid-brew. Dishwasher safe, though handwashing keeps the glaze looking new longer. Pairs with any standard #2 paper filter, no proprietary filters to reorder."

The second version ranks better because it naturally includes searchable terms real buyers use ("fits any mug," "standard filter," "dishwasher safe"), and it converts better because it answers the exact hesitations someone has mid-purchase: will it fit my setup, will I need special filters, can I clean it easily.

## Structure that helps both search and skimmers

A good product description isn't one paragraph, it's a short, scannable structure:

### Opening line: the specific benefit

Lead with the single most useful, specific thing about the product, not a vague claim. "Holds a charge for 14 hours of continuous use" beats "long-lasting battery life" every time, for both a search engine and a skimming customer.

### A short list of what's actually different

Two to four bullet points covering fit, materials, or use case differences that matter, not generic features every competitor also has. If your product is genuinely better because of one specific thing (a wider base, a stronger stitch, a longer warranty), say that plainly instead of burying it in adjectives.

### One line addressing the risk

Buyers hesitate over what happens if it doesn't work out. A single line about your return policy or a guarantee, even just linking to it, closes a real objection instead of ignoring it. If you've already worked on [reducing checkout friction](/blog/reduce-abandoned-checkouts-shopify), this is the product-page equivalent: removing a reason to hesitate before it costs you the sale.

## Where the SEO actually comes from

Keyword stuffing doesn't help anymore, and it never really did for conversion. What helps is using the actual language customers use, which usually isn't the language marketers default to. "Sweatproof" ranks and converts better than "moisture-wicking technology" for a lot of audiences, because it's what people actually type into Google and think in their own head.

A fast way to find this language: read your own reviews and support tickets. Customers describe products in their own words there, words you can often lift almost directly into your description. ## The mistake of writing once and never revisiting

Product descriptions written at launch reflect what you thought mattered before you had any customer data. Six months in, you have reviews, support tickets, and return reasons that tell you exactly what people were confused or hesitant about. Revisiting descriptions with that information usually improves both conversion and search ranking more than writing new pages from scratch, because you're now answering real, observed hesitations instead of guessed ones.

A simple habit: every time a support ticket asks a question the product page should have answered, add that answer to the description. Over a few months, your product pages start closing objections before they ever reach your inbox, which is the same instinct behind proactively catching problems before they cost you a weekend.

## What to cut

Cut adjectives that don't carry information: "amazing," "premium," "perfect," "must-have." None of these help a search engine understand the product, and none of them answer a real hesitation. If a sentence would still make sense with the adjective removed, the adjective wasn't doing anything.

## Bottom line

A product description that ranks and converts isn't longer or more polished, it's more specific. Answer the real question a buyer has, use the language they'd actually search for, and update the page as you learn what people are actually confused about. That's a better SEO strategy than most keyword tools will give you, and it closes more sales along the way.
