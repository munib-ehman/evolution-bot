Customer UGC is a photo, video, or written demonstration created by a real customer using your product. A small store can collect it consistently without an influencer budget by asking after the customer has had time to use the product, giving a specific creative prompt, securing reuse permission, and publishing only content that represents the product accurately.

The difficult part is not asking people to “tag us.” It is building a process that turns a scattered tag or email attachment into permissioned, organized media your product pages and marketing can actually use.

## Decide what evidence you need

Start with the customer question the content should answer.

| Product question | Useful UGC prompt |
|---|---|
| How large is it? | Show it beside a common object or in the intended space |
| How does it fit? | Share size, usual size, and a front/side view |
| How is it used? | Record the setup or first use from start to finish |
| What does the color look like? | Photograph it in natural light without a filter |
| Does it solve the stated problem? | Show the before, process, and realistic result |

“Send us content” is vague. “Show how the organizer fits in your smallest drawer” gives the customer an idea and gives the store usable evidence.

Choose one product family and one content goal for the first month. A small focused collection is easier to brief, review, and measure than a catalog-wide contest.

The [product-page mistakes guide](/blog/product-page-mistakes-that-kill-conversions) helps identify the visual questions your current product media does not answer.

## Ask after the customer can genuinely use the product

Trigger the request from delivery or expected use, not merely the order date. The right delay depends on the product:

- a simple accessory may be ready for a photo soon after delivery
- apparel needs enough time to try the fit
- skincare or consumables may need repeated use before a useful demonstration
- gifts may need to wait until the occasion

Use one primary request and, at most, one reminder. Stop after the customer submits, opts out, or asks not to receive another request.

A practical email:

> **Subject:** Show us how you use [product]
>
> If you have used your [product], we would like to see [specific scene or detail]. A phone photo or short vertical video is enough; no polished setup needed.
>
> Submit it here: [upload or reply method]
>
> Before we publish anything, we will confirm where we may use it and how you want to be credited.

Keep the submission path short. A reply email, supported review form, or dedicated upload page can work. Collect only the information needed to identify the product, contact the creator, and record permission. If the same post-purchase flow also asks for a review, keep the rating request neutral and separate from the media permission.

For review timing and wording, use the [product review collection guide](/blog/how-to-get-more-product-reviews).

## Give a small shot brief, not a production assignment

Ask for:

- one clear photo or a short vertical video
- natural light where possible
- the product visible and used as intended
- no beauty filter or edit that changes product color or result
- no private documents, addresses, children, or bystanders without appropriate permission
- one sentence about the use case, size, or setup

Do not demand several angles, a scripted testimonial, raw files, and a long caption in exchange for a small coupon. If you need a professional production, hire and pay for professional production.

The goal is credible customer evidence, not a cheaper imitation of a studio shoot. The [AI product photography guide](/blog/ai-product-photography-for-shopify-stores) covers controlled catalog imagery; customer UGC serves a different job and should not be edited until it looks synthetic.

## A tag is not reuse permission

Seeing your brand tagged in a public post does not automatically give the store permission to download the photo, place it on a product page, crop it into an ad, or keep using it indefinitely. The U.S. Copyright Office's [guidance on photos you did not take](https://www.copyright.gov/engage/docs/photography.pdf) says that using someone else's online photo requires permission.

Ask in writing and record the answer. Your permission request should state:

- the exact content covered
- the channels where you want to use it, such as product pages, email, organic social, or paid ads
- whether you may crop, caption, or lightly edit it
- how the creator will be credited, if at all
- the duration and territory of use
- whether the creator can withdraw future use and how
- whether compensation or a gift is involved

A concise request might say:

> We would like permission to use this photo on our product page, organic social accounts, and email for 12 months. We may crop it for layout but will not alter the product's appearance. We will credit [handle/name] as agreed. Reply “I agree” if these terms work for you, or ask us to change them.

Use terms reviewed for your business and jurisdiction, especially for paid advertising, minors, health claims, or perpetual licenses. Store the permission record beside the asset, not in an employee's personal inbox.

## Handle incentives and disclosures honestly

If a customer receives a discount, gift, free product, contest entry, or payment in exchange for creating or posting content, that relationship can be material to the audience.

The FTC's [endorsement and review guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/endorsements-influencers-reviews) explains the need for truthful endorsements and clear disclosure of material connections. Meta's current [Instagram branded content guidance](https://www.facebook.com/help/instagram/616901995832907?locale=en_GB) says a free or loaned product is an exchange of value and requires the paid partnership label for branded content.

Tell the creator what disclosure is required before they post, and review the live content. Do not assume a platform tool alone satisfies every legal requirement or audience context.

Never condition a reward on a positive opinion, five-star rating, or removal of criticism. If you offer the same small thank-you for usable content regardless of sentiment, say so plainly. Keep the content incentive separate from any customer-service remedy.

## Review each submission before publishing

Use a checklist:

- Permission covers the intended channel and edit
- The creator and product can be verified
- The product is shown accurately and used safely
- No unsupported performance, health, environmental, or comparative claim appears
- Required incentive disclosure is present
- Personal information and unapproved people are absent
- Music, artwork, logos, or other third-party material is cleared
- The file quality works for the intended placement

Do not publish a dramatic result merely because it performs well on social. If the result is atypical, unsafe, or unsupported, the content can mislead customers and create returns or complaints.

## Turn approved UGC into useful product media

Create an asset record with:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Asset ID | UGC-2026-014 |
| Product and variant | Blue travel organizer, small |
| Creator | Approved display name or handle |
| Source | Email submission |
| Permission | Product page, email, organic social; 12 months |
| Incentive | $10 store credit, disclosed |
| Expiry | 2027-08-29 |
| Status | Approved |

Shopify supports images and videos as product media, as described in its current [product media documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/product-media). Publish the asset where it answers a buying question rather than placing every submission in a generic gallery.

Add accurate alt text that describes what matters in the image. For example: “Customer wearing the medium rain jacket over a sweatshirt” is more useful than “happy customer.” Keep the original file and permission record even if you crop a storefront version.

Approved UGC can also support social conversations, but keep consent and attribution intact when moving between channels. The [Instagram comments and DMs guide](/blog/instagram-comments-dms-into-sales-shopify) covers the sales follow-up after a post creates questions.

## Measure the system, not likes alone

Track:

- requests sent
- submissions received
- permission rate
- approval rate
- time from submission to publication
- percentage of approved assets actually reused
- product-page behavior before and after placement
- support questions or returns related to the question the UGC was meant to answer

A high submission count is not useful if permission is missing or the assets never reach a product page. A smaller library mapped to real customer questions can do more work.

Start with your five highest-traffic products and list one visual question each page fails to answer. Send a precise request to recent eligible customers for one product, secure written permission for every asset, and publish the best piece where that question appears in the buying journey.
